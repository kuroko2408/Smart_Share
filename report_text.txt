Section 1 | Abstract
Smart Share is a mobile-first platform that helps people discover others whose journeys are compatible with
theirs, so that they can share a cab instead of booking separate ones. People leaving the same station, airport,
event or business district at about the same time, heading in a similar direction, rarely know about each other.
Smart Share lets a user post a ride request (pickup, destination, departure window, seats, and the maximum extra
minutes they accept) and returns a ranked list of compatible requests with an explanation of why each was
suggested.
The matching engine is location-aware and time-aware. It uses PostGIS to shortlist nearby pickups, then checks
departure-window overlap, travel direction and seat capacity. It evaluates the valid pickup and drop-off orders for
a pair, using OSRM road-network durations (with a clearly labelled approximate fallback), and keeps only pairings
that stay inside each rider's detour limit. The stack is React, TypeScript and Vite on the front end; Python, FastAPI
and SQLAlchemy on the back end; PostgreSQL with PostGIS hosted on Supabase; and Supabase Auth with
backend JWT verification.
Expected benefits are lower individual fares, better seat utilisation and fewer separate trips. These are expected
benefits and have not been measured. Smart Share currently produces ranked suggestions; it does not book
cabs, assign drivers or process payments.

Section 2 | Problem Statement
In dense cities such as Mumbai, many people start journeys from the same place, at nearly the same time,
towards the same or neighbouring destinations. Yet each person typically books a separate cab, because there is
no easy way to find out who else is going their way.
Typical situations:

• Railway stations (for example Dadar or CSMT): many commuters leave the same exit within minutes of each
other.
• Airports (for example Mumbai T2): arriving passengers head to nearby suburbs in a similar direction.
• Events and functions: a college festival or wedding ends, and hundreds of guests leave at once.
• Busy commercial areas (for example BKC): office crowds travel to similar residential corridors in the evening.
Informal coordination through messaging groups is manual and unreliable. Even when two travellers find each
other, they cannot easily judge whether sharing is practical: how far apart the pickups are, whether their departure
times line up, whether one rider would face a long backtracking detour. Straight-line closeness is misleading in
Mumbai; the project design notes show that two pickups at the same station can head in opposite directions
(Dadar to Colaba versus Dadar to Borivali), and that water bodies make nearby points far apart by road.
The consequences are unnecessary separate journeys, higher individual costs, underused vehicle
capacity and added congestion near crowded venues.

Section 3 | Proposed Solution
Smart Share connects riders through ride requests. A user signs in, creates a request, and asks the system to
find compatible requests from other users.

• Create a ride request. Choose a pickup and a destination (the current UI offers Mumbai landmark presets
such as BKC, Airport T2, Powai, Lower Parel, Dadar and Andheri West).
• Select a departure window (earliest and latest acceptable departure).
• State seats needed and the maximum extra minutes the rider will accept.
• Find matches. The backend discovers candidates, filters them and evaluates routes.
• Review ranked suggestions with score factors, each rider's estimated detour, the stop order and a
routing-accuracy notice.

How candidates are found: the engine shortlists active, unexpired requests from other users within 2 km of the
pickup, with overlapping departure windows and combined seats of at most four. It then rejects pairs travelling in
clearly different directions, evaluates the valid pickup/drop-off orders using road-network durations, and discards
any order in which a rider's extra time exceeds their own limit. Survivors are scored and the top five are returned.
Scope note. The documentation does not describe automatic cab booking, driver assignment or payments as
implemented, and this report does not claim them.

Section 4 | Objectives
Technical objectives
• Discover compatible passengers with location-aware spatial queries (PostGIS geography, GiST index).
• Apply time-aware matching through departure-window intersection.
• Evaluate direction and destination compatibility, and reject backtracking pairings.
• Evaluate valid pickup/drop-off orders and keep each rider's detour within their stated limit.
• Keep results explainable, deterministic and clearly labelled where routing is approximate.
• Establish a scalable foundation: separated modules, replaceable routing provider, migrations and automated
tests.

User-impact objectives
• Make it easy to find fellow travellers, and reduce reliance on separate individual cab journeys.
• Improve use of available seats.
• Minimise unnecessary detours for every rider.
• Offer an accessible, mobile-first experience with a simple request form.
• Help riders understand why a suggestion was made.
The user-impact objectives are design goals. The project's original pilot targets (for example 30% of eligible
requests matched, 20% estimated savings) have not yet been tested and are not reported as achieved.

Scoring model (as implemented)
Composite score = 30% pickup proximity + 30% destination similarity and direction + 20% departure-time

are broken by lower total detour, then smaller pickup distance. Early design drafts used five factors; the weights
above are the implemented ones.

Phase 0 - Planning and documentation
Six foundation documents: project charter, SRS/PRD, architecture and technical design, database and API
contracts, roadmap, and developer guide. Together they froze the MVP scope and set the matching approach as
a rule-based, explainable heuristic.

Phase 1 - Foundation
Repository, React/Vite/TypeScript frontend skeleton, FastAPI backend skeleton, environment configuration and a
health endpoint. The roadmap records a running local environment and CI configuration. The detailed Phase 1
audit was not among the documents reviewed, so details such as the Docker/PostGIS setup should be added by
the team.

Phase 2 - Database and Supabase integration
SQLAlchemy models and the first Alembic migration (5b69062ab3ef) create profiles and ride requests on
hosted PostgreSQL 17.11 with PostGIS 3.3.7. Locations are stored as geography(Point, 4326) with GiST indexes,
and database constraints enforce valid windows, 1-6 seats, detour limits and valid status values. Supabase Auth
is integrated, and the backend verifies ES256 tokens through JWKS.

Phase 3 - Ride request management
Authenticated create, list, view, update and cancel endpoints with ownership checks, coordinate and window
validation, and matching React forms and listings. Requests move through ACTIVE, MATCHED, CANCELLED,
EXPIRED and COMPLETED states.

Phase 4 - Matching engine (4A design, 4B build)
Phase 4A specified the algorithm and its worked Mumbai examples. Phase 4B implemented candidate discovery,
direction and capacity gating, enumeration of pairwise stop orders, per-rider detour checks, scoring, ranking,
three matching endpoints, the routing adapters and the 'Find matches' UI.

Section 10 | Innovation
Smart Share's contribution is not simply splitting a cab fare. It is deciding which strangers can practically
share a journey, and showing why. It combines several checks that simple proximity matching lacks:

• Location-aware pickup discovery using spatial indexing.
• Time compatibility through shared departure-window overlap.
• Direction and destination compatibility, so a rider heading north is never paired with one heading south.
• Multiple valid pickup/drop-off sequences, with riders required to share the cab for part of the trip.
• Detour-aware feasibility: each rider's extra time is measured against their own limit.
• Capacity constraints as a hard gate.
• Ranked, explainable results, with honest labels when routing is approximate.

• Economic: sharing a cab could reduce what each rider pays, and fills seats that would otherwise travel empty.
• Environmental: where pooling replaces separate trips, fewer vehicle trips could mean less fuel use and fewer
emissions.
• Social: easier discovery of fellow travellers and simpler coordination, potentially making transport more
accessible.
• Operational: better use of existing vehicle capacity.
None of these benefits has been measured. Realising them depends on user adoption, on fare logic still to be
built, and on matches converting into real shared trips, which the current version does not handle.
