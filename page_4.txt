Section 2  |  Problem Statement
In dense cities such as Mumbai, many people start journeys from the same place, at nearly the same time,
towards the same or neighbouring destinations. Yet each person typically books a separate cab, because there is
no easy way to find out who else is going their way.
Typical situations:
  Railway stations (for example Dadar or CSMT): many commuters leave the same exit within minutes of each
other.
 Airports (for example Mumbai T2): arriving passengers head to nearby suburbs in a similar direction.
 Events and functions: a college festival or wedding ends, and hundreds of guests leave at once.
 Busy commercial areas (for example BKC): office crowds travel to similar residential corridors in the evening.
Informal coordination through messaging groups is manual and unreliable. Even when two travellers find each
other, they cannot easily judge whether sharing is practical: how far apart the pickups are, whether their departure
times line up, whether one rider would face a long backtracking detour. Straight-line closeness is misleading in
Mumbai; the project design notes show that two pickups at the same station can head in opposite directions
(Dadar to Colaba versus Dadar to Borivali), and that water bodies make nearby points far apart by road.
The consequences are unnecessary separate journeys, higher individual costs, underused vehicle
capacity and added congestion near crowded venues.
